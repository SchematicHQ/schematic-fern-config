# Pricing model framework

Distilled from Schematic's published pricing guides (AI agent pricing models, software monetization models, credit burndown versus pay-as-you-go), anonymized patterns from hundreds of pricing consultations, and the attributed expert corpus - Kyle Poyar's Growth Unhinged and Rob Litterst's PricingSaaS, distilled 2026-08 from their most recent posts and reports. Use this file to map interview evidence onto candidate models. Cite the user's evidence when you recommend, and credit the named source when a claim below carries one (`references/sources.md` has the rule).

## The taxonomy

### Flat-rate subscription

One recurring fee for access. Fits when usage and cost to serve are steady and predictable per customer. Fails for AI products: agents retry, run concurrently, and consume compute without a human in the loop, so a flat fee caps revenue while cost scales - the classic margin squeeze. A flat platform fee survives best as the base of a hybrid.

### Per-seat

Price per human user. Fits collaboration tools where value tracks headcount and every seat logs in. Fails when value decouples from headcount - and AI decouples it hard: an autonomous agent does not occupy a seat, and a five-person team can drive more agent work than a fifty-person one. Seats also punish adding teammates, which fights product-led growth. Keep seats, at most, for platform access and admin controls, never for autonomous work.

The 2026 debate, credited: Kyle Poyar (Growth Unhinged) reads seats as "not future-proof" - his 2026 survey found investor preference has flipped to variable models (usage, outcome, hybrid) over flat and seat models by roughly 85 to 15. Rob Litterst (PricingSaaS) pushes back on seat maximalism with the Figma case: bundling AI credits per seat produced 80%+ weekly credit adoption among $10K+ customers, 136% net dollar retention, and gross margin up 2.5 points - the seat surviving as the predictability container with metering only at the top. His Docusign teardown shows the boundary: where automation does the work and users live in other tools, seats tax the wrong thing, and Docusign moved those enterprise deals to pure consumption credits with unlimited users.

### Tiered plans (good / better / best)

A packaging layer, not a metric: two to four plans that bundle features, limits, and support. Almost every model below ships inside tiers. The diagnostic question is what separates the tiers - a real value metric, or an arbitrary feature split customers cannot map to their own growth.

### Usage-based (pay-as-you-go)

Bill on raw consumption after the period: tokens, API calls, compute time, queries, GB. Fair by construction - heavy users pay more - and it protects margin when cost tracks the same meter. Transparent when one metric dominates. The costs: revenue and invoices swing with spikes, meters multiply as features ship, and buyers fear the unbounded bill. Two AI variants: **per-action** (each task; transparent, but commodity price pressure as inference gets cheaper) and **per-workflow** (each completed multi-step process; better value framing, harder to price when runs vary).

### Credit-based (burndown)

Customers buy or receive an allotment of credits and burn them per action, with different actions costing different amounts. This is the dominant landing zone for AI features, for reasons that repeat across consultations:

- **Credits translate technical units into a buyable currency.** Non-technical buyers cannot budget tokens or API calls. One abstract unit with a published action-cost table restores predictability.
- **Credits insulate the price from provider cost swings.** Model costs change monthly; repricing a credit table is easier than repricing a contract.
- **One pool spans features.** Chat, file processing, and search draw from the same balance, so new features monetize without a new meter.
- **Prepaid credits smooth revenue** compared to raw pay-as-you-go, and unused-credit policies (expiry versus rollover) tune the economics.

Watch for: opacity ("what does a credit buy?") - show balances, action costs, and consumption inside the product; and failed-work charges - hold credits when a task starts, commit on success, release on failure.

Credited context: Kyle Poyar's 2026 survey measured credit models growing 126% year over year, at 29% adoption with another 33% planning them within a year - and still calls credits "a lifeline, not the endgame," because juggling per-vendor credit currencies burdens customers. Rob Litterst's credit-launch checklist (PricingSaaS) adds the operating rules this skill treats as defaults: publish the dollar conversion and a real rate card; when model costs shift, adjust the rate card, never the headline subscription price; expect to add rollovers as the model matures; and make exhaustion moments offer options, not forced upgrades. His n8n teardown states the granularity law: the broader the agent's job, the finer the pricing unit is forced to be - narrow, predictable jobs earn coarser, more valuable units.

### Outcome-based

Charge per delivered result: resolved ticket, qualified lead, completed booking. Strongest value alignment and hardest to displace - and out of reach for most of the market, because it needs an outcome both sides can define, measure, and attribute. Recommend only when the interview shows a countable, attributable result the customer already treats as the product's job.

Credited context: Kyle Poyar pegs outcome-pricing adoption at just 5% and gates success on his CAMP prerequisites - consistency, attribution, measurability, predictability. George Kats (guest on PricingSaaS's Good Better Best) supplies the mechanics: billable outcomes should be boring, observable, and repetitive ("appointment booked" beats "pipeline created"), and every launch should be backtested by replaying historical events into would-have-been invoices. Rob Litterst's realistic bridge is **execution-based pricing** - charging for runs, workflows, and skill tiers as a layer on the existing model - which reframes the upgrade conversation from "you hit a limit" to "you want more done".

### Value-based

A pricing philosophy more than a mechanism: set price from the customer's gain (time saved, labor replaced, revenue added), segmented by who gains more. In practice it lands as segment-differentiated tiers or negotiated enterprise pricing anchored on a value story ("this replaces an analyst"). Requires willingness-to-pay research; recommend as the anchor for enterprise conversations, not as the self-serve mechanic.

### Freemium / free tier

An acquisition layer: a small standing allotment (often a few credits) that lets prospects feel the product. Recommend when the goal is new logos or self-serve motion; skip when every free unit carries real inference cost and the funnel cannot absorb it. Pair with top-ups so a free user can pay without subscribing.

### Add-ons and top-ups

Expansion mechanics on top of a base model: modules sold separately, and credit top-ups - a la carte packs or an auto rule ("when balance dips below X, buy Y"). Auto top-ups convert overage anxiety into self-serve expansion, and consultations show top-up revenue is often the difference between a flat account and an expanding one.

### Hybrid

A base platform fee plus a variable component (usage, credits, or outcomes). The most common recommendation for a reason: the base buys predictable revenue and covers fixed cost, the variable side captures upside as usage grows, and buyers get budget stability without a growth ceiling. The cost is operational: two meters, more explanation, real billing infrastructure. Most companies that "move to usage" actually land here.

Credited context: hybrid is the measured consensus, not just this skill's default - Kyle Poyar's 2026 survey found it the most popular model at 37% of companies, up from 25% a year earlier, and PricingSaaS's Q2 2026 Trends Report describes the same seats-to-hybrid migration with roughly 1 in 5 tracked companies making an AI pricing change in a single quarter. Poyar's "platform plus tokens" variant - a value-priced platform fee with cost-priced token pass-through - is the emerging answer where credits blur cost and value.

## Market benchmarks, credited

Numbers to deploy in conversation, with their sources and vintage (2025-2026):

- Hybrid pricing: 37% adoption, up from 25% the prior year; the most popular model (Kyle Poyar, 2026 State of B2B SaaS and AI Monetization).
- AI credit models: grew 126% year over year; 29% adoption, 33% more planning within 6-12 months (Poyar, same survey).
- Outcome pricing: 5% adoption despite the noise; Salesforce and HubSpot announced outcome models in 2026 (Poyar).
- Median target gross margin on AI capabilities: roughly 50%, versus the 70-80%+ SaaS norm (Poyar).
- The top 10% of power users consume 70-80% of AI credits or tokens - the cohort metering exists for (Poyar).
- Pricing shelf-life compressed from roughly 18 months to as little as 6; three in four companies changed pricing within a year (Poyar).
- 14.6% of 4,060 tracked companies show an AI agent on their pricing page; 70% bundle it free; only 16 repriced an agent in a quarter - adoption first, monetization later (PricingSaaS, State of Agent Monetization, Q2 2026).
- Losing about 20% of deals on price is healthy; near-zero pushback means priced too low, 40%+ means the market moved (Poyar's rule of thumb).
- Enterprise threshold mechanics can multiply an AI bill overnight - one 150-employee company faced a $400K to $1.4M jump crossing a vendor's enterprise seat threshold (Poyar's reporting).

## Entitlements: what the model asks someone to enforce

Every model above implies entitlements, and a model nobody can enforce is a wish. An entitlement is what a customer is allowed to do. It is not the same as what they are billed for, and the gap between the two is where pricing breaks: an unenforced limit is a price list that describes something the product does not do.

Four distinctions decide whether a recommendation is buildable:

- **Where the grant comes from.** A plan, an add-on, or a per-customer deal. A model that needs the same feature entitled all three ways is fine; one that needs code changes to move a feature between them is not.
- **Pooled or per-user.** A company-level allowance is simpler and the common starting point. Per-user enforcement is what stops one power user from draining a shared balance, and it is usually a later, larger piece of work.
- **Hard or soft limits.** A hard limit blocks the action. A soft limit permits it and bills, warns, or flags. Enterprise buyers usually want a hard cap available even when they never set one, because the option is the thing that gets the deal past procurement.
- **Runtime or after-the-fact.** Checking entitlement at the moment of the action prevents overspend. Reconciling it at invoice time only reports overspend. Agentic and high-concurrency products need the first, and a hold-then-commit ledger on top of it.

Ask what the user can enforce today before recommending a model that depends on enforcement. When the answer is "nothing yet", the instrumentation becomes phase one of the roadmap rather than an assumption inside it. `references/requirements-library.md` carries the full requirement themes; this section is the part that shapes a recommendation.

## Diagnostic mapping

Map interview evidence to model signals. Multiple signals stack.

| Evidence from the audit | Points toward | Points away from |
| --- | --- | --- |
| AI feature with real inference cost per action | Credits, hybrid | Flat rate, per-seat |
| Usage spiky or seasonal | Credits (pooled, rollover), hybrid base for stability | Pure PAYG (bill shock), flat rate (margin risk) |
| Buyers asked for spend caps or not-to-exceed | Credits with caps and auto top-up rules | Uncapped PAYG |
| Value metric known and charged on today | Deepen the current model, add expansion mechanics | Wholesale model change |
| Value metric known but not charged on | Move the meter to it (usage or credits on that unit) | Keeping the legacy metric |
| Expansion today comes mostly from renegotiation | Self-serve expansion: top-ups, overages, tier ladders | Static custom contracts |
| Enterprise buyers, procurement, fixed budgets | Hybrid with committed base plus drawdown, custom plans | Pure month-to-month PAYG |
| Cost to serve varies 5-10x between similar customers | Usage or credit component priced on the costly dimension | Flat rate |
| Countable, attributable business result | Outcome component (small, alongside a base) | - |
| Goal: new logos, self-serve motion | Free tier plus low-friction entry plan plus top-ups | High entry minimums |
| Goal: margin proof | Metered component tied to the cost driver | Unlimited plans |
| Non-technical buyers | Credits or per-workflow units | Token or API-call meters |
| Broad, general-purpose agent | Finer units - tokens or metered credits with strong meter UX | Coarse per-action prices (PricingSaaS's n8n lesson) |
| Narrow, repeatable, attributable job | Per-action or execution pricing; outcome candidate if CAMP holds | Raw token metering |
| "We don't know" on usage or cost instrumentation | A roadmap phase for metering before any usage launch | Launching usage pricing now |

## Consultation patterns, from the calls

How Schematic actually advises, mined from recorded prospect and customer calls (Oct 2025 - Aug 2026: ~170 advice-shaped matches triaged, 25 full summaries read, 5 transcripts consulted). Anonymized - situations generalized, no companies, no deal terms. Counts are how many of the 25 read calls show the pattern. Cite these as "Schematic's consultation corpus".

1. **Sell visibility and governance WITH the meter** (11/25). Enterprise buyers resist usage and credit models over surprise-bill fear. Ship the model with an auditable ledger, burn-rate views, org caps, per-user limits, and ~80% alerts - and demo the governance during the sale. Controls turn a hit limit into an expansion conversation instead of a billing dispute.
2. **Value metric first, model second** (8/25). Teams ask "which model?" before knowing the unit of value that scales with customer success. The metric is the hardest element to change later; the model is the delivery mechanism around it.
3. **Credits over pay-as-you-go for AI sold into contracts** (8/25). For AI agents and assistants sold to contract buyers, advise a credit grant or annual commit with per-action burn rates - the middle path between cost-passthrough token billing and rarely-operable outcome pricing. The credit forecast doubles as a negotiation lever, including re-packaging the same economics as a flat annual deal when procurement demands it.
4. **Soften the seat meter with a credit layer** (5/25; hybrid base-plus-usage broadly: 9/25). Seat products with inactive-seat friction keep seats as the predictable base (often granting credits per seat) and add user-initiated top-ups that bypass admin approval; where inactivity is the sore point, committed-base-plus-overage or pay-per-active-seat.
5. **Run structured WTP interviews before any change** (6/25). Five to ten structured 30-45 minute willingness-to-pay interviews - product immersion, relative value, value metric, packaging reaction, price ceiling - with contacts NOT in an active sales cycle. Primary research replaces founder bias and builds the organizational confidence that is the real repricing blocker.
6. **Meter and simulate before committing** (5/25). Instrument usage, then simulate candidate models against real historical usage of the largest accounts; or pilot the model on one negotiated deal; or launch with monthly resets to learn consumption before annualizing.
7. **Platform fee plus credits for early-stage AI** (5/25). Pre-seed and seed AI products with no pricing yet: a platform fee anchors predictable revenue (what investors underwrite), credits protect margin against expensive models - and expect to revise the model with roughly every new customer cohort.
8. **Reprice through the rate card, never the headline** (4/25). Define a credit currency deliberately not mapped to tokens, publish per-action burn rates, and manage margin by adjusting burn rates as model costs shift. The public price stays untouched; customers accept burn-rate evolution while delivered value holds and the ledger stays transparent.
9. **Offer procurement a menu** (4/25). On enterprise deals with unknown procurement preferences, present two or three structures (pay-as-you-go, volume tiers, flat) built on the same value metric and priced off the same credit forecast. Lose deals over value misalignment, never over model shape.
10. **Keep the outcome in the narrative, bill on credits** (4/25). Founders wanting outcome pricing keep the outcome in discovery and the price level, not the meter - outcome attribution becomes a per-customer debate, and metering the outcome changes customer behavior against the vendor (one observed charge-on-acceptance attempt raised the buyer's acceptance bar and suppressed usage).
11. **Reprice stagnant legacy pricing at a version boundary** (4/25). Decades-old flat or seat pricing gains a usage metric that scales with the customer's own growth (rates declining at volume so scale never feels like a tax), shipped with a major version: old version keeps the old price, the new one carries the new model.
12. **Bill the unit with the tightest cost/value correlation** (4/25). Where candidate units vary by orders of magnitude, bill on the correlated dimension (duration over count) and weight actions with different burn rates - flat per-unit pricing over high-variance units misprices both sides.
13. **Hold the annual commit, pace it monthly** (4/25). Sell the annual commitment, enforce monthly pacing framed as a budgeting feature with self-service override; a hit pacing limit triggers a resize conversation, not an empty tank. Long-running jobs use the hold/commit/release ledger pattern so parallel agents cannot overdraw one balance.
14. **Diagnose sales enablement before repricing** (4/25). Long cycles blamed on pricing: first review recent sales calls for confident value discovery and dress-rehearse the pricing pitch on an outsider - hesitation explaining the model is itself the diagnostic. Repricing cannot fix an enablement gap.

## Choosing and pressure-testing

1. Define the job the product performs (action, workflow, outcome, or access) - the natural unit follows from it.
2. Name the customer value created and pick the metric that tracks it.
3. Check unit economics at low, average, and heavy usage against known costs.
4. Match the buyer: technical buyers tolerate raw meters; everyone else needs credits, workflows, or outcomes; enterprise needs predictability and controls.
5. Pressure-test with real customers: walk the best customer and the spikiest customer through the candidate model and check both bills feel earned (the year-two "you earned the uplift" test from the interview).

Mistakes to name when you see them coming: a billing unit buyers cannot understand, charging for failed work, seat pricing on autonomous work, one flat fee across wildly different customer sizes, and hiding usage, limits, or overage rates outside the product.

## Attributed claims from the summit sessions

### Kyle Poyar, The State of AI Pricing in 2025
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6-ju8lOv87c

- Survey data from a year ago, across companies with AI offerings. Roughly half priced on flat fees or seats. 20 percent were usage-based, 3 percent outcome-based, 27 percent hybrid. Almost none expect flat fees or seats to be primary in three years. [benchmarks]
- Undercharging for AI now has a real cost. In one company the top 5 percent of users drove about 75 percent of usage cost. They paid a flat fee, so they were deeply unprofitable. [usage-based]
- Hybrid is where the market is converging, most often a platform fee plus credits. Seats no longer track value when a product delivers more as fewer people log in. [hybrid]
- Each step from on-premise to subscription to usage to outcome moves risk onto the vendor. In exchange the vendor captures more of the economic value created. On-premise models captured roughly 5 percent of that value. [outcome-based]
- Outcome pricing creates a moat for the market leader. A cheaper competitor with a lower success rate nets the customer less money, so price alone cannot win. [outcome-based]
- Pick outcome metrics you control and can measure quickly, such as resolved conversations or completed work products. Revenue or cost savings take months to attribute and stall the deal. Agree on the definition and its measurability before signing, because attribution disputes are where outcome deals break. [outcome-based]

### Marcos Rivera, Is Consumption Pricing The Only Viable Model for AI?
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QpuBqF0QCAg

- Consumption is the destination but there are several routes. Ordered by complexity: access tiers, flat plus unit price, three-part tariff, credit systems, multi-axis credit systems, pay as you go. Most companies are converging on the three-part tariff and credits. [usage-based]
- Two tests for a consumption metric. If customers are working to find a way around it, it is probably wrong. Buyers also judge the model by its worst exchange, meaning the single unit where they got least for what they paid. [usage-based]
- Overage anxiety is the central problem to solve. Three levers help. Protection means caps. Performance means throttling or queueing instead of billing. Passive means deferring the charge or truing it forward into renewal. [usage-based]
- Credit hygiene has four rules. Define the unit and its state, such as action completed versus action processed. Keep credit types under three. Match the reset period to the billing period. Reward long commitments with extra credits and overage leniency, not only a discount. [credits]
- Price credits off variable cost rather than fully loaded cost. Volume spreads fixed costs, and switching models can change your unit economics overnight. [credits]
- Pure per-unit pricing races to the bottom. Differentiation lives in the base access layer, through capabilities, exclusive data sources, bundled services, or integrations nobody else has. For developer tools, a 10 to 20 percent markup on passed-through costs is tolerated and 40 to 60 percent is not. [packaging]

### Daniel Chalef, Using Metering to Land & Expand Enterprise AI Deals
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=f_8SUfsWr6A

- An advertised low-dollar self-serve price anchors the enterprise conversation. A visible 499 dollar monthly plan invites doubt about whether you are an enterprise vendor. Metered billing breaks that anchor, because buyers extrapolate from a unit price to a large number themselves. [enterprise]
- Metered billing is self-limiting at scale. It gets expensive enough for large accounts that they ask for volume pricing, which is the natural opening for an enterprise contract. [enterprise]
- Contracted limits are negotiation surfaces. Let the customer exceed the contract. Then trade an overage discount for something you want, such as an annual term instead of a quarterly one. [enterprise]
- Free plans for business software should be thin. A generous free tier bought tens of thousands of low-quality signups that were not useful. Free exists to reduce experimentation friction, not to give the product away. [packaging]
- Choose a metric the buyer already thinks in. Megabytes were technically precise but developers do not size text in megabytes, and the number read as expensive. [packaging]
- Metered billing has two failure modes at the low end. It collapsed the bottom of the business from 99 to 375 dollars per month down to 2 or 3. A base fee with usage included is the fix. It also invites unpaid bills, so charge the card at a low threshold to validate it early. [usage-based]

### Gaurav Vohra, How To Roll Out Major Price Changes Correctly
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=e9xwkSmd1Qs

- A long-deferred price increase was the single largest year-over-year revenue driver. It worked as both a one-time migration lift and a permanent rate change. It waited years because of prioritization, not analysis. [price-increases]
- Force-migrate existing customers upward. Create the lower tier by removing two or three years of accrued innovation. That is far cheaper for engineering than inventing new features for a higher tier. [price-increases]
- Communicate by email only, roughly three months ahead, with about one reminder. Skip in-app banners. Observed results: about 5 percent took the downgrade and under 10 percent took the annual lock-in. Churn ran 1 to 2 percent above baseline for 60 to 90 days. [benchmarks]
- Do not assemble a cross-functional pricing committee. One or two people closest to the problem should drive the change, because a committee is the slowest way to execute it. Pricing A/B tests will not reach significance below very high pricing-page traffic, so roll out rather than test. [price-increases]
- The Van Westendorp question that matters is the price at which a buyer would still purchase but it feels expensive. Segment the answers hard. The same product drew 15 to 20 dollars from operators, 30 from founders, and 45 from investors. [willingness-to-pay]
- Never put an individual-to-team price step in a product-led motion. Every tier should be team-capable, and higher tiers carry a higher per-seat price, not a lower one. Shared billing alone is chargeable value. Free tiers need a justification, either competitive pressure or a compounding network or data advantage. [packaging]

### Simon Ooley, Selling AI into the Enterprise
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ypW_cKsj-yE

- A credit or a token has no inherent value. It gains value only when tied to the customer's desired outcome and to how many are consumed to reach it. [credits]
- Predefined good, better, best packages no longer define value for horizontal AI products. The customer can do almost anything. Feature-bounded packages leave value on the table and repel buyers who do not see themselves in a tier. [packaging]
- Scope every enterprise deal as a use case with four components: actors, workflow, consumption metric, and success criteria. Credits are the currency that delivers the use case. [enterprise]
- Model as many use cases as possible, sell the top few, and keep the rest as a ranked upsell backlog. Unsold use cases become the expansion pipeline, and use-case-level consumption data makes forecasting progressively more accurate. [enterprise]
- Use-case ROI beats a blanket multiple. A CFO will not work through a 400-question ROI questionnaire but will follow one use case to a number. Procurement then negotiates outcomes rather than price, because spending less means getting fewer use cases delivered. [enterprise]
- The modeled use case doubles as the implementation blueprint. That fixes the analysis-paralysis onboarding problem common to horizontal workflow products. [enterprise]

### Panel with Vercel, Stripe, and Schematic, How to Build Billing That Can Keep Up With AI
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=t2eOKrMDJMA

- One panelist reported five to six pricing changes per month at a large infrastructure company, counting new SKUs, add-ons, and packaging changes. Annual repricing cannot keep up with AI. [benchmarks]
- Four things slow pricing changes. Multiple sources of truth for price. Weak cost and usage observability once SKUs sprawl. Hardcoded entitlements. Non-standardized checkout, trial, and add-on flows. [ai-monetization]
- Keep the self-serve pricing catalog as the single source of truth and make the sales-led motion read from it. A quoting tool that becomes a second catalog causes incidents and blocks new price types. [ai-monetization]
- Serve usage and cost from a single endpoint. Let customers tag usage along dimensions you do not price on, such as department, so they can run their own cost observability. [usage-based]
- Entitlements need four properties. Decoupled from pricing. Overridable by go-to-market and finance. Cycled independently of the invoice cycle. Audit-logged. Gate features behind a primitive that returns a boolean plus usage and allocation, so plan and subscription never appear in application code. [ai-monetization]
- Three metrics say whether billing architecture is working. How fast you can change pricing, which should be under a week. Billing uptime, which should be five nines. The share of support tickets that are billing-related. [benchmarks]

### Gio Hobbins, How to Design Credit-Based Pricing That Builds Trust (and Revenue)
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9dypE8fKcU8

- Credits give both sides cash-flow predictability, hide variable consumption rates behind one currency, and compose well alongside seat-based and usage-based pricing. [credits]
- Trust requires four disclosures. What a credit is worth. What each action costs. How actions have burned down the balance. What happens at the limit. Most credit products, coding tools especially, never show the action-level ledger. [credits]
- Unused credits are more visible than unused subscription value, and that visibility drives cancellations. Partial or same-month rollover softens it. A badly designed credit economy feels like a casino where every action spends and nothing is earned back. [credits]
- Onboarding needs a seatbelt. New users routinely burn an entire trial allocation in minutes on one oversized job. Size trial credits by working backwards from the aha moment, then multiply by the repetitions needed before a user feels hooked. [credits]
- Revenue recognition on credits is constrained by performance obligations. Unused prepaid credits cannot be recognized until the obligation is met or the grant expires. Standalone bundles therefore need expiration dates, and included, promotional, and purchased credits must be tracked separately. [credits]
- Do not price credits as cost-plus alone. Anchor the credit to the job it completes or the human labor it replaces, which is a much stronger story than internal cost. [credits]

## Anonymized patterns from the therapy sessions

Nine working sessions with founders and operators. Counts say how many of the nine showed each pattern.

- A founder sets the first price from a position of desperation, then never revisits it. The advice is to put pricing on a review cadence and treat it as part of the product. Product value accrues silently, so the gap between price and value widens every quarter. The correction eventually becomes the biggest growth lever available. Seen in 5 of 9 sessions.

- A company hardcodes entitlements as plan checks scattered through the application code. The advice is to model the smallest sellable unit instead. That is a boolean access grant, a metered quantity, or a time-boxed grant. A plan then becomes a composition of those units. Pricing logic is a cross-cutting concern and is very hard to test. Once it is sprayed across a codebase, every packaging change becomes a multi-week change. Seen in 3 of 9 sessions.

- A team delivers a well-researched pricing strategy that the business then cannot implement. The advice is to run discovery on the current systems in parallel with the strategy work, not after it. Retrofitting a strategy onto an unexamined billing and entitlement stack either stalls the project or produces a rushed build. Seen in 3 of 9 sessions.

- A company appoints one pricing owner, usually under finance, and expects the practice to mature. The advice is to name a coordinator but distribute the work across product, product marketing, revenue operations, analytics, and sales leadership. When a single owner is needed, product or product marketing is the recurring recommendation. Packaging is the hard part, and packaging is a product decision. Finance sits too far from the customer to see value, and sales incentives conflict with long-run price integrity. The related failure is a product manager who can describe the roadmap but not the business model, because sprint capacity persuades nobody outside engineering. Seen in 6 of 9 sessions.

- A seat-priced tool finds that value no longer tracks headcount. Automation or single sign-on often removes the per-user logic. The advice is to find a metric that scales with delivered value, then deliberately leave seats uncapped so teams can onboard freely. Capping seats adds friction to exactly the expansion motion you want. Seen in 5 of 9 sessions.

- A product-led company gates too much behind the paywall and leaves too large a gap to the first paid tier. The advice is to give a taste of every layer rather than withholding whole layers. Add an intermediate step when the entry price is a large jump. Users who cannot reach value in the free product will not pay to find out. Seen in 3 of 9 sessions.

- A packaging change lands while enterprise deals are in flight. The advice is to pick a policy deliberately. Either freeze in-flight deals from the change, or use the change as a deadline to accelerate close. Relaxing a limit on a self-serve tier can also nuke the upgrade reason a rep is selling on. Sales needs advance notice either way. Seen in 3 of 9 sessions.

- A stakeholder arrives asking to drop the price because competitors look cheaper. The advice is to check whether it is a communication problem first. Remember also that raising price sheds unprofitable customers on purpose. Price cuts are one-directional and hard to reverse. A customer base can hold a stale impression long after you changed the price. Seen in 3 of 9 sessions.

- A team invests heavily in quantitative willingness-to-pay research and treats the number as the deliverable. The advice is to spend most of the effort on how you charge. That means offer design, the price metric, and the segment each option targets. The number falls out of the offer, and the offer is what customers actually evaluate. Seen in 4 of 9 sessions.

- A company wants to test pricing but fears putting the quarter at risk. The advice is to route a fixed percentage of new deals to the new structure. Feedback then arrives in weeks without exposing the whole pipeline. The hardest part is not the data. It is aligning leadership and sales on acting when results are ambiguous. Seen in 3 of 9 sessions.

- A company acquires or ships enough new products that the offer combinations multiply. The advice is to rationalize packaging before the sales motion calcifies, because reps default to selling only what they already understand. Combination growth is exponential, not linear. The accumulated mismatch behaves like debt on every future pricing decision. Seen in 3 of 9 sessions.

- An AI product tries to set price against model costs that move underneath it. The advice is to route different features to different model tiers. Model a range of cost scenarios rather than a single point. Project cost declines without assuming them. Per-token prices fall sharply, but smarter models do more work and emit more tokens, so cost per job does not fall as predictably. Seen in 2 of 9 sessions.

## How these documents are structured

### The recurring anatomy

All six open with a context section before any requirement appears. It restates the buyer's current
stack, commercial model, and constraints in the buyer's own framing. Four of the six invite explicit
correction of that restatement. This is the single highest-value section: it is cheap to write, and
it surfaces misunderstandings before they become scoped work.

All six define a status legend up front, before the first table. The legend is short, usually one
line per status.

All six then break into themed sections. Each section carries a one-line scope statement saying what
it covers and, in the better documents, which situation raised it.

All six use a three-column table as the atomic unit:

| Column | Contents |
| --- | --- |
| Requirement | One capability, stated from the buyer's side, testable |
| Response | How the approach under evaluation addresses it, concretely |
| Status | One of four values from the legend |

Column order varies. The triple does not.

Four of the six close with sections that are worth copying:

An assumptions section, listing what was inferred from context rather than stated directly. Each
assumption says why it matters and what to confirm.

An open questions section, listing what needs a working session rather than a written answer.

A topics-not-yet-discussed section, flagging areas that usually come up for a company at this stage
but did not come up on the calls. One document used this to surface trials, refund policy on
consumed usage, self-serve upgrades, and multi-currency.

A phased implementation path, splitting requirements into what ships first and what follows.

A status count summary, tallying how many requirements fall into each status, per section and
overall.

Two of the six include inline correction notes, marking exactly where a previous version of the
document was wrong. This is a strong signal of a document being used rather than filed.

### What makes a requirement line well-formed

A well-formed line is one capability. If a row contains "and", check whether it is two rows.

A well-formed line is testable. Someone should be able to write a pass or fail check against it
without asking what it means.

A well-formed line names its unit. Per seat, per credit, per event, per company, per billing period.
A requirement about usage that does not name a unit cannot be answered.

A well-formed line names the actor and the moment. Who is enforcing, and when: before the action
runs, at the end of the period, or at invoice time.

A well-formed line carries the business situation that produced it. The situation is what lets a
responder propose an alternative that solves the problem better than the literal ask.

A well-formed line separates now from later. Phase one requirements and phase two aspirations
answered on the same page produce a scoping argument later.

Compare these pairs:

| Vague | Well-formed |
| --- | --- |
| Flexible pricing | Change the price of a tier, and the composition of its feature set, without an application deploy |
| Supports usage-based billing | Meter one event per completed document, aggregate per billing period, and pass the quantity to the invoice as its own line item |
| Good reporting | Query raw consumption events by company, actor, feature, and date range through an API, and export the result to a warehouse |
| Enterprise ready | Single sign-on for administrative access, an audit log of every entitlement change with actor and prior value, and a current security certification |
| Handles our enterprise deals | A sales representative configures a per-customer plan with non-standard limits and a bundled services package, without engineering involvement |

### A note on inference

The strongest of the six documents marked which requirements were inferred rather than stated. It
gave each inference a reason it mattered and a question to close it. Requirements written only from
what a buyer said out loud will miss the areas the buyer has not thought about yet. Writing the
inferred ones down, clearly labeled, is what turns a transcript into a document.

## The requirement themes

Seventeen themes recurred. Each carries the count of documents that raised it, out of six.

### 1. Plan and package architecture

Raised in 5 of 6.

Covers how many things a customer can buy at once, how those things compose, and who can change the
composition.

The situation: a vendor selling more than one thing under a single contract. Typically a base
subscription, one or more separately-sold add-on products, and occasionally one-time services. The
question underneath is whether the catalog can be reshaped by the commercial team, or whether every
packaging change is an engineering ticket.

Requirement lines drawn from the six:

- A customer carries exactly one base plan, with recurring and one-time add-ons attached to the same
  company record.
- A feature can be entitled through the base plan, through an add-on, or through a per-customer
  deal. Moving it between those three is a configuration change.
- A tier ladder where each tier carries its own feature set, limits, and price, typically free,
  self-serve paid, and sales-led.
- The free tier is expressed in the same primitives as the paid tiers, not as a separate code path.
- A customer expands from one tier to the next, or attaches a new product, without re-contracting
  from scratch.
- Selective feature removal on a negotiated deal: the customer buys the tier minus one named
  feature, configured rather than coded.
- Adding, removing, or reshaping a tier is a configuration change owned by product or go-to-market.
- Plan-level entitlements and add-on entitlements coexist on one customer and resolve to a single
  answer at the check.
- One consolidated invoice covers the base subscription, the add-ons, and any one-time services.
- The feature, not the SKU, is the atomic unit of the catalog. A plan is then a collection of
  features, each carrying its own monetization rule.

### 2. Seat licensing and volume tiers

Raised in 4 of 6.

Covers per-seat and per-headcount licensing, and the pricing curves that go with them.

The situation: a vendor whose price scales with the size of the customer. Large deployments pay
materially less per unit than small ones. The seat count also moves during the term.

Requirement lines drawn from the six:

- Per-seat licensing tracked as a numeric count on the contracting company, with users inheriting
  entitlement from the company.
- A volume curve stepping the per-seat price down as seat count rises, applied automatically from
  the quantity rather than quoted by hand.
- Separate curves per tier, so the same seat count prices differently on a higher tier.
- Annual or monthly billing cadence chosen per customer against a single catalog.
- Prorated charges when licenses are added mid-term.
- A recurring platform fee plus volume-tiered per-seat fees on the same plan.
- A one-time setup fee charged at signature alongside the recurring components.
- Multi-year terms with either locked pricing or a defined ramp across years.
- A per-headcount metric billed on the customer's active population each period, rather than on
  purchased seats.
- Seat counts that decrease as well as increase, with enforcement following the decrease.

### 3. Usage metering and consumption allowances

Raised in 6 of 6.

Covers what gets counted, at what rate, against what allowance.

The situation: a vendor with a variable cost per action. Most commonly that is model inference, but
equally workflow executions, documents processed, or messages delivered. This theme appeared in
every document, including the ones whose commercial model was otherwise entirely
subscription-based.

Requirement lines drawn from the six:

- Meter an arbitrary unit per event, with the unit definition changeable without re-instrumenting
  the application.
- Switch the metered unit from an input proxy such as tokens to an outcome such as documents
  processed, as a configuration change.
- An allowance included in the plan, defined per company or per seat, refreshing each billing
  period.
- Consumption beyond the included allowance priced per unit, or drawn from a prepaid balance.
- Different actions burn at different rates against the same allowance, with the rate set per
  action.
- Burn rates adjust independently of customer-facing prices, as the underlying cost of goods moves.
- Aggregation windows aligned to the billing cadence, plus shorter windows such as daily or weekly
  for budget control.
- Idempotent consumption keyed on a caller-supplied identifier, so a pipeline retry does not
  double-count.
- Negative or reversing events that return consumption after a failed, retried, or disputed action.
- Events carrying both a company identifier and an actor identifier, where the actor may be a system
  rather than a person.
- Headless and agent-to-agent traffic metered on the same path as interactive product usage.
- A registration schema for new billable actions: identifier, display name, description, fixed or
  variable cost, default cost, category.

### 4. Credits, wallets, and ledgers

Raised in 5 of 6.

Covers prepaid balances, how they are sold, how they are spent, and how they are accounted for.

The situation: a vendor selling consumption in advance rather than billing it in arrears. Buyers
increasingly expect the pattern they already know from large model providers and developer tools:
buy a pack, watch it draw down, top up. Two documents debated expiring monthly blocks against
rolling purchased packs, and both concluded the rolling pack generates fewer support tickets.

Requirement lines drawn from the six:

- Prepaid packs in a few fixed sizes, plus a negotiated custom size granted through an API.
- Larger packs carry a lower price per credit, so the pack size is the discounting instrument.
- Purchased credits roll over and expire a fixed interval after purchase.
- Plan-included credits reset at period end and do not roll over.
- A defined burn order across grant types, typically soonest-expiring first, so period-scoped
  credits burn before purchased ones.
- Payment taken at purchase against the stored payment method, rather than accumulating to an
  end-of-period invoice.
- Automatic top-up when the balance crosses a threshold, with retry on failure and a notification on
  repeated failure.
- A spend cap that pauses automatic refills once a period total is reached, set at company or user
  level.
- An append-only ledger of grants, debits, holds, adjustments, and expirations, queryable per
  company.
- The ledger identifies which grant each consumed credit came from, so cost basis and breakage stay
  recoverable.
- A stated policy on refunding credits already consumed, and on chargebacks against consumed usage.
- Real-time balance readable in the product, split into included and purchased buckets with their
  expiry dates.
- A translation from credits to work, rendered from configuration, so the product can state what one
  action costs.

### 5. Entitlement enforcement: limits, latency, and failure behavior

Raised in 6 of 6.

Covers the mechanics of the check itself.

The situation: consumption that costs the vendor real money on every action, so discovering an
overage at invoice time is not acceptable. Every one of the six raised this, and the two most
technical documents devoted their largest sections to it. This is where a requirements document
stops being a commercial artifact and becomes an engineering one.

Requirement lines drawn from the six:

- A synchronous check before the action executes, returning both access and remaining balance in one
  call.
- Check latency low enough for the critical path, stated as a typical figure and a tail percentile.
- State held locally or in-network, so a check does not require a round trip on every call.
- Hard limits that block the action at the ceiling, and soft limits that notify while allowing it.
- A configurable buffer between soft and hard, so the stop lands slightly past the contracted amount
  rather than exactly on it.
- Concurrent draws against one balance that cannot race it negative.
- A reserve, commit, release pattern for variable-cost actions: hold an upper bound, commit the
  actual, release the remainder automatically.
- A configurable hold timeout per action type, so a short interactive action and a long batch job
  carry different windows.
- Defined behavior during service degradation, configurable as fail-open or fail-closed per check or
  per customer segment.
- Defined behavior for actions already in flight when a limit is reached.
- Limit changes taking effect without an application deploy or a manual cache flush.
- Enforcement logic living outside application code, with no plan identifiers hardcoded in the
  product.
- Pre-flight cost estimation, so the product can tell a user what an action will cost before they
  commit to it.

### 6. Custom enterprise deals and per-customer overrides

Raised in 6 of 6.

Covers the machinery for deals that do not fit the catalog.

The situation: a sales-led motion where no two large contracts are identical. Without good override
tooling, the catalog fills with near-duplicate plans. Every document raised this. It is the most consistently underestimated theme, because it looks like an edge case
and behaves like the main path.

Requirement lines drawn from the six:

- A per-customer plan starting from a template, adjusting features, limits, and pricing for one
  deal.
- Overrides that bump a single limit or grant a single feature on top of a standard plan, without
  forking the plan.
- A justification note captured on every override and every plan change, for downstream review.
- Optional expiry dates on overrides, so a temporary exception reverts on its own.
- Bundled service entitlements sold in the contract: a support tier, a named account manager, a
  quantity of training hours.
- One-time service charges attached to the same contract as the recurring subscription.
- A sales representative configures and quotes the deal without engineering involvement.
- Order form line items converted into the per-customer plan, ideally drafted from the contract
  language rather than retyped.
- Non-standard deals landing in a draft state awaiting human approval, while fully standard deals
  auto-provision.
- Pay-on-invoice terms with a defined grace period before access is cut off.
- Grants of extra allowance to a specific customer ahead of a renewal conversation, without a plan
  change.

### 7. Customer hierarchy, pooled quotas, and multi-entity governance

Raised in 5 of 6.

Covers what happens when the contracting entity is not the same as the consuming entity.

The situation appears in two distinct forms. The first is the large enterprise buyer with multiple
business units that wants one invoice and separate tracking. The second is a marketplace or
platform. One party buys a pool, and people with no membership relationship to that party draw it
down. Both produce the same set of questions.

This was the most common source of gap and roadmap statuses across the six documents.

Requirement lines drawn from the six:

- One billing relationship spanning several internal divisions of a customer, producing a single
  consolidated invoice.
- Usage and limits tracked separately per sub-organization while the invoice stays consolidated.
- An explicit decision on whether limits are pooled across sub-organizations or set per
  sub-organization against a shared billing entity.
- Group-level allocation, where each division receives a named share of a purchased pool.
- A parent-level view aggregating balances and consumption across children.
- Splitting a single purchase across child organizations from the parent level.
- Pooled quota at the company level with per-user visibility, so heavy users are identifiable for
  governance or expansion.
- Per-user enforcement, so no single user can consume the entire pooled allowance.
- A precedence order across levels, where a per-user override beats a role default, which beats the
  organization default.
- Role or group defaults, so different job functions carry different per-user limits.
- A pool bought by one party and drawn down by individuals with no fixed relationship to that party.
- Consumer identity carried as event metadata rather than as an identity linked to the buyer, to
  avoid an identity explosion in a many-to-many model.
- Time zone for limit reset boundaries, configurable per organization rather than fixed to one zone.

### 8. Billing system integration and division of responsibility

Raised in 6 of 6.

Covers which system owns what, and what breaks when the answer is wrong.

The situation: every one of these buyers already had a billing stack, and none was replacing it
wholesale. The recurring question was not which billing system to use, but which system sits in the
billing engine role. One document made this its central architectural recommendation. It named, line
by line, which capabilities collapse when the entitlement layer has no billing engine behind it.
Per-customer paid plans become unbilled entitlement sets. Tiered and volume pricing collapse to
static numeric limits. Metered usage can be counted but not invoiced. Credits become a
non-monetized ledger.

Requirement lines drawn from the six:

- A clear split naming which system owns subscription state, which owns invoicing, and which owns
  the entitlement decision.
- The entitlement layer able to run purely as metering and enforcement, with purchase events
  injected from an upstream system.
- Metered usage flowing into billing as invoice line items, one line per metered feature with its
  quantity.
- Grants created through an API by whichever system records the purchase.
- Proration, upgrades, and downgrades executed by the billing engine, triggered by the entitlement
  layer.
- Checkout or payment links generated from the negotiated terms against the existing customer
  record.
- Tax assessment at checkout and on the invoice, by the billing engine or a tax partner.
- One customer record per contracting company across systems, or an explicit decision to use several
  that roll up.
- A sandbox environment wired to a sandbox of the billing system.
- A named list of what is lost if the preferred billing engine is not in the loop.
- A decision on whether an existing payment-gateway-only account is reused or a fresh billing
  account is opened.
- Bidirectional flow, so contract edits and exceptions made in the billing system reach the
  entitlement layer rather than being overwritten.

### 9. Plan versioning and grandfathering

Raised in 3 of 6.

Covers changing prices without breaking signed contracts.

The situation: a vendor iterating on pricing while existing customers hold terms they signed under
an earlier catalog. The three documents that raised it were the three with the largest existing
customer bases. The three that did not raise it were earlier-stage, or launching a greenfield
product. This theme appears to arrive with scale rather than at the start.

Requirement lines drawn from the six:

- Multiple plan versions live at once, with existing customers remaining on the version they signed.
- New customers landing on the current version automatically.
- Per-customer rates surviving a catalog-wide rate change, so a legacy contract keeps its negotiated
  burn rate.
- Version changes that do not re-rate historical consumption.
- A diff view showing exactly what changed between two versions.
- Bulk migration of a cohort between versions, with an audit trail preserved.
- Migration scheduled for the end of a customer's billing period rather than applied on the spot.
- An explicit decision on whether legacy plans are carried forward into the new architecture or
  sunset during migration.

### 10. Migration off an existing system

Raised in 4 of 6.

Covers replacing whatever is doing this job today.

The situation varied across the four. One had a feature-flag tool that solved the on-off layer but
not the monetization layer. One had a homegrown entitlement table fed by a CRM through a custom sync
service. One was replacing a legacy subscription billing tool. One was replacing an invoicing tool
as part of a finance re-platform. In every case the existing system was load-bearing and could not
be switched off on a single date.

Requirement lines drawn from the six:

- An inventory of existing flags classified by type, boolean, numeric, or event-based, so the port
  is mechanical rather than interpretive.
- Boolean flags porting directly, with numeric and consumption primitives layered on afterwards.
- The product rollout flag system and the entitlement system converging, so the team maintains one
  abstraction instead of two.
- Net-new customers onto the new stack first, then a backfill, rather than a single bulk cutover.
- A dual-write transition window, where the legacy store runs as a read replica fed by the new
  system.
- An explicit decision to retire the legacy store, with a date, rather than leaving it running
  indefinitely.
- Existing customers moved as renewals come up, with old subscriptions cancelled as the new ones
  activate.
- Historical purchase and account context imported as company attributes, with billing history left
  in the finance stack.
- A greenfield product line used as phase one, keeping the migration off the critical path for
  launch.

### 11. Provisioning, identity, and the contract-to-access handoff

Raised in 3 of 6.

Covers turning a signed contract into working access without a human retyping it.

The situation: a sales-led vendor whose CRM holds the closed-won deal and whose product holds the
entitlements, with a manual step between them. Two of the three documents corrected an earlier claim
here, noting that the integration was a documented workflow recipe rather than a managed connector.
That correction is worth imitating: the difference between a connector and a recipe is real
implementation work.

Requirement lines drawn from the six:

- A closed-won deal in the CRM triggering company creation and plan assignment.
- Standard deals provisioning hands-off, with non-standard deals stopping for human approval.
- Field mapping from the deal record to the company record defined explicitly rather than inferred.
- Companies identified by the vendor's own keys, with several keys resolving to one company.
- Conflicting keys for a single company producing an error rather than a silent merge.
- Company creation behaving as an upsert, so an unknown company is created and a known one updated.
- A display name distinct from the legal entity name carried on the contract.
- Deprovisioning at the contract end date when the contract does not renew.
- An honest statement of which direction data flows, and whether the reverse direction exists at
  all.
- Line-item parsing from the contract object, where the shape of that object determines how cleanly
  it can be automated.

### 12. Admin console, roles, and commercial self-sufficiency

Raised in 5 of 6.

Covers who can change a customer's entitlements, and how.

The situation: sales and customer success needing to provision, adjust, and troubleshoot without
filing engineering tickets. One document made this its entire phase one. The buyer described the ask
as a screen listing the entitlements, with buttons to turn them on. Another framed it as
commercial agility: the ability to run pricing experiments and cut custom deals without pulling
engineering into every change.

Requirement lines drawn from the six:

- A console showing a customer's plan, features, limits, current consumption, and balance on one
  screen.
- Changing a customer's plan from the console, effective immediately in the running product.
- Turning a single named entitlement on or off for one customer.
- Role-based access, so sales, finance, support, and revenue operations see and do different things.
- Environment-scoped and resource-scoped permissions rather than one global administrator role.
- An audit log of every administrative mutation recording who, what, when, and the prior value.
- Consumption history per customer, shaped for a renewal or expansion conversation.
- Engineering registering a new billable action without vendor involvement or a central review gate.
- Webhooks on plan changes, limit events, and threshold crossings, for internal alerting and CRM
  enrichment.
- Programmatic parity, where everything the console does is also available through an API.
- Administrative single sign-on, with an interim invite flow if the directory integration lands
  later.

### 13. Customer-facing surfaces: purchase, portal, and notifications

Raised in 4 of 6.

Covers what the end customer sees and can do without talking to a person.

The situation: product-led expansion running alongside a sales-led motion, where the product itself
sells the next increment. The four documents that raised it were the four with a self-serve tier.

Requirement lines drawn from the six:

- A balance and consumption view inside the product, split by grant type, showing reset or expiry
  dates.
- Warnings at defined thresholds of the allowance, delivered by webhook, email, and in-product
  banner.
- A zero-balance wall that deep-links to purchase rather than failing silently.
- Purchase completed on the payment method already on file, with no card re-entry.
- Self-serve top-up, seat addition, and tier upgrade without a sales conversation.
- Customer-configured automatic recharge with a customer-chosen threshold.
- Consumption rates rendered from configuration, so the stated cost of an action never drifts from
  the enforced cost.
- An upsell prompt triggered by repeat top-ups, signaling that the customer belongs on a larger
  plan.
- A decision between embeddable components and a custom interface built on the API.

### 14. Access lifecycle: trials, grace periods, free tiers, and deprovisioning

Raised in 5 of 6.

Covers the moments when access starts and stops, which are rarely the moments money moves.

The situation: sales-led onboarding where access is granted at signature and paid later, and free
tiers where access exists with no payment at all. One document described the failure mode plainly:
without enforced revocation on non-payment, customer success turns into collections.

Requirement lines drawn from the six:

- Access activated at contract signature with a defined grace period before first payment.
- Automatic loss of access on non-payment, enforced by the entitlement layer rather than by a human
  process.
- A free tier modeled as a real plan with real limits, not an unmodeled default.
- An explicit decision on whether the free tier can purchase consumption or must upgrade first.
- Trial length, trial entitlements, and whether a payment method is captured up front.
- The conversion path from a free or early-access cohort onto a paid plan.
- Time-boxed exceptions that expire automatically instead of persisting unnoticed.
- Deprovisioning at contract end, and a defined experience for the customer after it.
- Delayed billing activation, so a plan can be live before the first invoice is due.

### 15. Reporting, analytics, and revenue recognition

Raised in 5 of 6.

Covers who reads the consumption data afterwards, and what they need from it.

The situation: three different audiences reading the same events. Finance needs recognition and
deferred revenue. The commercial team needs expansion signals and renewal risk. The end customer
needs to see their own consumption. Documents that treated these as one requirement produced worse
answers than documents that separated them.

Requirement lines drawn from the six:

- Raw consumption events retained and queryable by company, actor, feature, and time period, not
  only pre-aggregated rollups.
- Export of consumption, entitlement state, and customer data into a warehouse or business
  intelligence tool.
- An analytics API with parity to the vendor console, so custom dashboards are possible.
- Per-grant cost basis and expiration data, so deferred revenue and breakage can be computed.
- Performance obligation data written into the billing system, so recognition runs where finance
  already works.
- Named ownership of revenue recognition, accounts receivable, and the close.
- Attribution of consumption back to the individual actor, for reporting to the paying customer.
- Retention windows for consumption data and for audit data, with an enterprise extension available.
- Commercial reporting for account teams covering adoption, expansion signals, and renewal risk.
- A per-user consumption table, both in the vendor console and in any embedded customer-facing view.

### 16. Audit, compliance, and security posture

Raised in 5 of 6.

Covers what happens when a procurement team or an auditor reads this system.

The situation: selling into regulated buyers, where the entitlement system is in scope for both a
security review and a financial audit. One document tied it directly to bank security
questionnaires; another to the immutability requirements a finance team places on a consumption
ledger.

Requirement lines drawn from the six:

- An immutable append-only record of consumption, where corrections post as new entries and existing
  entries are never rewritten.
- Configuration change history recording which limit changed, by whom, and from what value to what
  value.
- Audit data queryable by date, company, actor, and feature through an API, not only in a console.
- Stated default audit retention and the maximum available on an enterprise agreement.
- Single sign-on for administrative access, and a stated position on driving roles from directory
  group membership.
- A current security certification, an encryption statement covering transit and rest, and a data
  processing agreement.
- Data residency by geography, stated honestly, including where it does not yet exist.
- A statement of whether payment card data is in scope, and which party carries that scope.
- Distributed tracing spans emitted for enforcement calls, so they appear inside existing traces.

### 17. Deployment, scale, and delivery reliability

Raised in 5 of 6.

Covers the operational profile of a component that sits in the critical path.

The situation: the entitlement check runs before user-visible actions, so its failure modes are
product failure modes. Two documents raised network-constrained deployments. A customer's security
posture forbids a persistent outbound connection, so enforcement has to work inside their network.

Requirement lines drawn from the six:

- Sustained and burst event volume stated as a number, with a comparable reference deployment.
- Latency stated separately for the cached path and the network path, each with a percentile.
- A self-hosted component running inside the customer's own network, for latency or data-residency
  requirements.
- A deployment mode for customers with no persistent outbound connection, using periodic sync rather
  than streaming.
- Delivery reliability in the client library: batching, retry, idempotency keys, and replay.
- A server-side event log used to confirm what actually arrived during integration and testing.
- An uptime target, incident response and resolution targets, and what a breach entitles the
  customer to.
- Separate development, staging, and production environments with environment-scoped keys.
- Client libraries covering every language and framework in the stack, generated from one
  specification.
- A backwards-compatibility policy and a stated deprecation notice period.
- Client-side inspection of events in flight, distinct from the server-side log, if engineers need
  it during integration.

## Status vocabulary

Every one of the six documents assigned a status to every requirement. Four statuses recur:

| Status | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Works today | Available now with no additional engineering. Configuration only. |
| Config required | Works, but needs setup or wiring: field mapping, plan modeling, a workflow in a connected system. No engineering. |
| On the roadmap | Scoped and in active development, with a delivery date someone will commit to. |
| Gap | Not solved today. Requires design work, a build decision, or an accepted workaround. |
| Open | Not yet assessed. Every row starts here. |

The source documents used vendor-facing labels for the same four states (Out of the Box, Config Required, Near-Term Roadmap, Gap / Needs Design). The wording above is what `assets/requirements-template.md` ships, and it is the one to use - a buyer's document should read in the buyer's voice.

Two variations appeared. One document split the fourth status in two. Needs Design means solvable,
but requiring a joint design conversation. Gap means not supported and not on the roadmap, requiring
a deliberate build decision. Another document added In Progress for work already under construction
but not yet released. Both refinements are worth adopting when the distinction is load-bearing for
the buyer.

### Why status beats a wish list

A wish list records what someone wants. A status-marked requirements document records what will
happen, which makes it usable as a plan.

It forces the honest answer. A row that says Gap is more valuable than a row that says yes with
qualifications buried in prose. Two of the six documents used Gap to describe things the vendor
could not do, and both were stronger for it.

It makes the shape of the risk visible. Counting statuses per section reveals where implementation
risk concentrates. In these six the counts clustered predictably. Enforcement and plan modeling ran
almost entirely Out of the Box. Hierarchy, provisioning integrations, and per-user granularity
carried nearly all the roadmap and gap entries.

It separates two different kinds of work. Config Required is scoping work with a known shape. Gap is
design work with an unknown one. Conflating them is how implementation timelines slip.

It converts a sales conversation into an implementation plan. Every requirement already carries its
status, so the phased plan writes itself. Everything Out of the Box goes in phase one. Config
Required items get owners. Roadmap items get dates. Gaps get either a workaround or a decision.

It creates an artifact both sides can be held to. Several of the six were explicitly framed as
pre-signature alignment documents, with an invitation to correct anything mislabeled. That framing
is what makes the status column meaningful rather than promotional.

A practical rule: if a requirements document has no status column, it is a wish list. Add the column
and the document starts doing work.

## Entitlements: the concept underneath

All six documents circle the same idea without always naming it. An entitlement is the answer to one
question: what is this customer allowed to do right now?

That question is distinct from what the customer is billed for, and keeping the two separate is the
core insight these documents encode.

### Allowed to do versus billed for

Billing is a periodic, backward-looking, financial record. Entitlement is a continuous,
forward-looking, operational decision. They are usually derived from the same contract and they
routinely diverge in practice.

A customer in a grace period is entitled but not yet billed. A customer who has stopped paying is
billed but should no longer be entitled. A customer granted a temporary exception before a renewal
is entitled beyond what they pay for. A customer on a legacy contract is entitled under terms the
current catalog no longer sells.

Systems that treat entitlement as a view of billing state cannot represent any of those cases
cleanly. Every one of the six documents contained at least one requirement that only makes sense if
entitlement is its own layer.

### Where an entitlement can come from

A single customer's effective entitlement usually resolves from several sources at once, in
precedence order:

The plan, which carries the baseline set for everyone on that tier.

The add-on, which attaches an additional capability to a customer without changing their plan.

The custom deal, which is a per-customer plan carrying negotiated features, limits, and price.

The override, which adjusts a single value for one customer on top of everything else. It often
carries an expiry date and a justification note.

The role or user level, where different people inside one customer carry different values.

Resolving these in a defined order is the thing that makes the model workable. When the precedence
is undefined, every exception becomes a new plan, and the catalog grows one plan per customer.

### Pooled versus per-user

An allowance held at the company level and drawn down by anyone is pooled. An allowance attached to
each individual is per-user. Most organizations want both: an organization-wide ceiling with
per-person limits underneath it.

Pooled is simpler and is where most implementations start. Its failure mode is concentration: one
heavy user consumes the shared allowance and everyone else is blocked. Documents in this set
addressed that in two stages, shipping pooled enforcement with per-user visibility first, then
per-user enforcement afterwards. Visibility without enforcement is a real intermediate step, because
it makes the problem diagnosable and gives the account team a conversation to have.

### Hard versus soft limits

A hard limit blocks the action. A soft limit permits it and raises a signal.

The choice is commercial, not technical. A hard limit protects the vendor's margin and the
customer's budget, and it interrupts the customer's work. A soft limit protects the experience and
transfers risk to the vendor. Most of these documents wanted both on the same feature. A soft limit
at the contracted amount triggers notification and upsell. A hard limit somewhat above it caps
exposure. The gap between the two is the overage buffer, and it should be an explicit configured
value rather than an emergent one.

### Runtime enforcement versus after-the-fact billing

The oldest model meters usage and reconciles at invoice time. It works when the marginal cost of an
action is near zero, so overage is a billing conversation rather than a loss.

Model inference broke that assumption. When one runaway loop can burn real money in minutes,
enforcement has to happen before the action, not after the period. That single change produces most
of the technical requirements in these documents. It drives the synchronous pre-action check and the
tight latency budget. It also drives the reservation pattern, the race-free concurrent draw, and the
defined degradation behavior.

The reservation pattern deserves a name because it recurs and is easy to miss. For an action whose
cost is unknown until completion, the caller holds an upper bound before starting. It commits the
actual amount at the end, and the difference is released automatically. Held amounts expire on a
timeout so a crashed process does not strand a balance. Without this, a system either blocks
conservatively on the worst case or permits overspend.

### The practical test

Here is a useful coherence check. Pick one customer and ask what they are allowed to do. Then trace
where each part of that answer came from. If the answer requires
reading application code, the model is in the wrong place. If it requires reading the invoice, the
two concepts have been collapsed. If it can be read from configuration and a ledger, the model is
doing its job.

## Interview questions that produce these requirements

Ask these of someone who has never written a requirements document. Grouped by theme. Not every
group applies to every business, and the answers to the first group determine which of the rest are
worth asking.

### Commercial model

1. What does a customer buy from you today, and can they buy more than one thing at once? Walk me
   through your largest contract line by line.

2. Which parts of your price scale with the size of the customer, and which are flat? If something
   scales, does the per-unit price change as the quantity rises?

3. What do you charge for that costs you money each time it happens? What is the unit you would
   want to charge for, as opposed to the unit that is easiest to count?

4. Does a customer pay before they consume or after? If before, do unused amounts roll over, and
   for how long?

5. When you sign a large deal, what in it is different from the catalog? Name the last three
   exceptions you made.

### Packaging and change

6. Who decides what is in a plan today, and what has to happen for that to change? How long does a
   packaging change take from decision to live?

7. When you raise a price, what happens to customers already on the old one? Are you willing to run
   two prices simultaneously, and for how long?

8. Is there anything you want to sell separately later that is bundled today, or the reverse?

### Limits and enforcement

9. When a customer hits their limit, what should happen? Does the action stop, or does it complete
   and generate a notification?

10. If the answer is that it stops: how far past the contracted amount is acceptable before it
    stops? Is that number configured, or is it whatever falls out of the implementation?

11. Do you know the cost of an action before it runs, or only after it completes? If only after, what
    is the plausible worst case for a single action?

12. Where does the check need to happen, and how much latency can that path absorb? What is on the
    other side of the check that the user is waiting for?

13. If the entitlement service is unreachable for two minutes, should the product allow the action
    or block it? Is that answer the same for every action and every customer?

### Hierarchy and identity

14. Who signs the contract, and who actually consumes the thing being sold? Are they always in the
    same organization?

15. Does any customer of yours have multiple business units that want one invoice and separate
    tracking? What does the largest one look like structurally?

16. Should one person be able to consume the entire organization's allowance? If not, do you need to
    enforce that, or is being able to see it enough for now?

17. Do different roles inside one customer need different limits? Who sets those, you or the
    customer's administrator?

### Existing systems

18. What sits in the billing engine role today: what holds subscription state, generates invoices,
    and takes payment? Are those the same system?

19. What decides what a customer can access right now, in production, today? A database table, a
    flag tool, a field on the account, hardcoded logic?

20. Where does a signed contract live, and what is the manual step between signature and working
    access?

21. What are you willing to replace in this stack, and what is non-negotiable? Which constraint is
    a real constraint versus a current habit?

### Operations and the people who use it

22. When a customer calls support asking why a feature is off, who answers, and what do they look
    at? Can they fix it themselves?

23. Who needs to change a customer's entitlements without an engineer? What should they be
    prevented from doing?

24. Who reads the consumption data afterwards, and what decision do they make with it? Ask
    separately for finance, for the account team, and for the customer.

25. What does an auditor or a security reviewer need from this system? Has a deal ever been slowed
    by that question?

### Closing questions

26. What has to be true at launch, and what can follow three months later?

27. What would you have to build yourself if the approach you pick does not do it? Is that
    acceptable, or is it disqualifying?
